# Music and sound design for mounting dread and approaching catastrophe (film prologues, trailers, documentary montages)

Scope: rules an automated system can use to cut archival montages to music and procedural sound. Researched 2026-10-27. Source quality varies: trailer "how-to" pages are vendor/tutorial marketing; composer facts come mostly from press interviews (several were 403 for direct fetch, so some facts rest on search-result summaries and are marked). Anything not directly sourced is under Inferences (marked INFERENCE) or Gaps.

## 1. Trailer music structure, tempo and cut-rate conventions

### Takeaway
The trailer convention is an escalating three-wave form: an atmospheric, sparse opening; a middle that raises stakes and energy; a final run of hits before the title card. Risers usually resolve into a hit (often a braam) or into a brief silence. Cuts land on musical hits and get shorter act by act, but cutting on every beat reads as robotic, so strong beats are saved for reveals and title cards. No reliable published BPM table exists; numbers below are inferences.

### Cited Findings
- Trailers are mostly built as escalating waves: a quiet atmospheric opening that sets mood, a middle that introduces stakes and gradually raises energy, and a final act that delivers a run of hits before the title. (Sound-library vendor guide.) — [Morphic: trailer sound effects](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/trailer-sound-effects); [Violet Recording: Sound design for trailers](https://violetrecording.com/sound-design-for-trailers/)
- Many trailers pair a riser with a braam; a riser is usually followed by a hit or a brief silence. — [Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/cinematic-sound-effects); [Ableton blog, Richard Veenstra (The Solos)](https://www.ableton.com/en/blog/learn-how-to-make-high-impact-sounds-for-movies-and-trailers/)
- A production-music track breakdown describes the trailer ending as an abrupt cutoff to silence that leaves only a short reverb tail ("hit, then silence" finish). — [filmmusic.io / Sascha Ende track notes](https://filmmusic.io/nl/song/12658-the-most-shocking-halloween-trailer-19)
- Trailer editing guides: every transition lands on a musical hit and the cut rhythm accelerates as the track builds; hold shots early, shorten them act by act, cut hardest on the hits; put the title card on the loudest moment after a deliberate beat of silence. (Tutorial-grade sources, not trailer-house interviews.) — [pexo.ai trailer guide](https://pexo.ai/tutorial/how-to-make-a-movie-trailer); [Flashboards: movie trailer](https://flashboards.yaroflasher.com/learn/editing/movie-trailer/)
- Cutting on the beat gives rhythm, but doing it too often becomes robotic; use strong beats for title cards, reveals and transitions. — [Epikton: pacing in trailers](https://epikton.net/a-quick-guide-to-pacing-in-trailers/)
- Game-trailer editor Derek Lieu: cutting on every music beat gets repetitive quickly; he mixes beat cuts with matching beats to on-screen motion. — [Derek Lieu, "My trailer editing thought process"](https://www.derek-lieu.com/blog/2018/1/6/what-im-thinking-when-i-edit-a-trailer)
- Trailer sound designer Bryan Jerden (Inception, The Dark Knight Rises trailers): silence is a strong statement that must be planned at the picture-edit stage and not used as a cliché. — [Designing Sound interview, 2012](https://designingsound.org/2012/07/19/exclusive-interview-bryan-jerden-on-trailer-sound-design/)
- The BRAAAM is a loud, low brass (real or synthetic) blast that became a 2010s trailer staple, used to mark something important, often on CGI collisions and fades to black; Indiewire (2013) called it nearly omnipresent; trailers using it include World War Z, Pacific Rim, Prometheus, Mad Max: Fury Road, Batman v Superman. — [Wikipedia: BRAAAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRAAAM)
- Braam origin is disputed: Zimmer says he played brass into a church piano with the sustain pedal held, plus electronics; Vulture reported bassoon, French horn, trombone and tuba on one note with timpani; Mike Zarin says it grew from a subway/train rumble effect in the teaser; Zack Hemsey ("Mind Heist") made it part of the music rather than an effect; earlier examples cited are Transformers (2007) and District 9 (2009). — [Wikipedia: BRAAAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRAAAM); [Northwestern Hundredth Mile: origins of braaam](https://sites.northwestern.edu/hundredthmile/the-origins-of-braaam); [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/braaams-beginners-how-a-horn-793220)
- Braam construction recipe: a long, loud, low brass note in the C1-C2 register, layered in octaves (C0, C1, C2), with automated distortion and reverb, optionally pitched down gradually. Hit recipe: five layers (distorted kick or taiko, high metal/percussive element, processed cymbal, sub, atmosphere), each EQ'd separately, followed by a long reverb tail. Riser: pitch plus filter automation with LFO speed and depth increasing together. Pitching recordings down 2-3 octaves works when the source has strong highs. — [Ableton blog (Richard Veenstra)](https://www.ableton.com/en/blog/learn-how-to-make-high-impact-sounds-for-movies-and-trailers/)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE, default macro-form for a 60-120 s dread prologue: Act 1 = 0-35% of duration (drone + sparse pulse, shot length about 3-6 s, cuts on bar lines or phrase starts); Act 2 = 35-80% (ostinato enters, shot length halves to about 1.5-3 s, cuts on beats 1 and 3); Act 3 = 80-95% (cuts every beat, then half-beats in the last 2-4 bars, hits on downbeats); a 0.5-1.5 s suck-out; final hit + title on the loudest moment; 2-5 s stinger/tail. The percentages come from the three-wave descriptions above, not from a measured corpus.
- INFERENCE, cut-rate acceleration: model shot length as a geometric decay (each act about 0.5x the previous act's median), clamped to a floor of about 8-12 frames at 24-30 fps for legibility of archival images. Archival stills/film need more frames to read than modern footage.
- INFERENCE, beat budget: put cuts on no more than about 50-70% of available beats in Act 2 and reserve the strongest downbeats (bar 1 of a phrase) for reveals, maps/titles and the turn, per the "not every beat" advice above. Off-beat cuts that follow on-screen motion keep the montage from feeling mechanical.
- INFERENCE, tempo: dread trailers commonly sit around 60-90 BPM in the opening and keep the same tempo while subdividing (8ths, then 16ths) instead of raising BPM; perceived acceleration comes from subdivision and cut density more than from a tempo change. Not verified by a corpus.

### Gaps
- No interview with a named trailer house (Trailer Park, Mark Woollen & Associates, Create Advertising) or with Two Steps From Hell / Audiomachine giving numeric structure (bar counts, BPM, timing of acts) was found in this session.
- No measured corpus of trailer BPM ranges or shot-length curves was found. A follow-up could measure a sample of trailers with beat tracking + shot detection.
- A Vox interview with trailer editor Bill Neil was mentioned in search results but not retrieved.

## 2. Tension devices and their documentary uses

### Takeaway
The core dread toolkit is: a continuous rising illusion (Shepard-Risset), a mechanical pulse (ticking watch, heartbeat) whose rate is manipulated, low drones/sub, rough or dissonant textures, and sounds built from the real place of the catastrophe (Chernobyl from a sister power plant). Documentary practice (Ken Burns' The Vietnam War) rebuilds ambience for silent archive, degrades or muffles it to match the image, and uses heartbeat-like pulses that speed up and slow down with the story.

### Cited Findings
- Dunkirk: Nolan sent Zimmer a recording of his own pocket watch; its ticking was turned into a synthesizer sound and the score was built from it. — [Gulf News: Dunkirk soundtrack](https://gulfnews.com/entertainment/dunkirk-soundtrack-how-it-all-came-together-1.2066116); [Daily Courier via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/canada/the-daily-courier/20170715/281934542995282)
- Nolan says he wrote the Dunkirk script around the Shepard tone principle, an illusion he had explored earlier with composer David Julyan on The Prestige; the score uses a tone that seems to rise continuously without arriving. Nolan described months of balancing "how much is too much", worrying the effect could turn audiences off. — [The Playlist (Business Insider interview)](https://theplaylist.net/nolan-hans-zimmer-dunkirk-score-20171130/); [Kottke: the infinite auditory illusion in Dunkirk](https://kottke.org/17/07/the-infinite-auditory-illusion-that-makes-the-dunkirk-soundtrack-so-intense-and-good)
- CBC headline reports Zimmer explaining audio trickery that made Dunkirk audiences nauseous (page returned 403; content not verified). — [CBC Q](https://www.cbc.ca/radio/q/blog/hans-zimmer-explains-the-audio-trickery-that-made-dunkirk-audiences-nauseous-1.4311684)
- Chernobyl (HBO, 2019): Hildur Guðnadóttir built the score almost entirely from recordings made inside the decommissioned Ignalina nuclear plant in Lithuania (a sister plant of Chernobyl and a filming location), with field recordist Chris Watson and producer Sam Slater; sounds included pumps, reactor and turbine halls and piping; the only traditional instrument was her voice, processed with impulse responses recorded on site. It won the 2019 Emmy for music composition (limited series). — [Universal Music Canada](https://www.universalmusic.ca/?p=91571); [uDiscover Music](https://www.udiscovermusic.com/?p=2131607); [Holland Festival](https://hollandfestival.nl/en/chernobyl); [The Vinyl Factory](https://thevinylfactory.com/?p=142262)
- Sam Slater described the plant as so large you would not hear reverb; Ignalina's reactors shut in 2004 and 2009. (Per search-result summary of Sound on Sound; direct fetch was 403.) — [Sound on Sound: Hildur Guðnadóttir & Sam Slater](https://www.soundonsound.com/people/hildur-gudnadottir-sam-slater)
- The Vietnam War (Burns/Novick, 10 episodes): Jacob Ribicoff was music editor and did about a third of the sound design. Archival footage rarely had usable sound, so ambience was rebuilt (foley, footsteps, jungle movement, night insects, birds over paddies, helicopters, gunfire). — [MPA: The Vietnam War sound designer](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/10/the-vietnam-war-sound-designer-on-enhancing-emotional-memories-of-the-war/)
- Ribicoff matched sound quality to image quality: grainy, degraded North Vietnamese footage (compared to WWI film) got sounds that were smeared, played very quietly and mixed low; more vivid American footage got fuller sound. — [MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/10/the-vietnam-war-sound-designer-on-enhancing-emotional-memories-of-the-war/)
- A multi-day session of Vietnamese voices (genders, ages) was recorded in New York for whispers through to crowd panic at the fall of Saigon. — [MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/10/the-vietnam-war-sound-designer-on-enhancing-emotional-memories-of-the-war/)
- For veteran John Musgrave's listening-post story, Ribicoff mixed helicopter blades with a heartbeat and made it fluctuate, speed up and slow down as the story built to its climax; for chaotic footage (night flares, shaky handheld bombing footage) he used echo, tones and invented sounds to match the visual energy, moving into an abstract, emotional sound space. — [MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/10/the-vietnam-war-sound-designer-on-enhancing-emotional-memories-of-the-war/)
- Ribicoff has worked with Ken Burns since Jazz (2000) (MPA) and on about 14 Burns documentaries (library profile, per search summary); he received 2018 Emmy nominations for The Vietnam War and HBO's Fahrenheit 451. — [MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/10/the-vietnam-war-sound-designer-on-enhancing-emotional-memories-of-the-war/); [East Brunswick Public Library profile](https://www.ebpl.org/taxonomy/term/25/about-us?page=142); [Film Independent](https://www.filmindependent.org/blog/detail-oriented-acoustic-perfection-with-sound-designer-jacob-ribicoff/); [Designing Sound / Pro Sound Effects](https://designingsound.org/2018/09/12/prosoundeffects-jacob-ribicoff-and-the-sound-of-hbos-fahrenheit-451/)
- Horror scores imitate nonlinear, rough sounds (like animal alarm calls/screams); Blumstein and colleagues analysed 102 film scores and found horror closely mimicked this rasping, unpredictable sound. (Original: Blumstein, Davitian & Kaye 2010, Biology Letters, as summarised in press.) — [Scientific American: how composers make horror music terrifying](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-composers-make-horror-movie-music-sound-terrifying/); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2023-10-score-expert-techniques-tension.html)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE, Shepard-Risset generator spec (standard construction; parameters are suggested defaults): 6-9 sine or band-limited partials spaced one octave apart under a fixed bell-shaped (Gaussian in log-frequency) amplitude envelope centred around 250-500 Hz; all partials glide up at a constant rate (suggest 1 octave per 8-20 s for slow dread, 1 octave per 3-6 s for the last act); each partial fades out at the top of the envelope while a new one fades in at the bottom. Layer it under the ostinato and raise its glide rate rather than its level as the catastrophe approaches.
- INFERENCE, ticking/heartbeat clock: start a tick or heartbeat at or near the music tempo (for example 60-72 per minute), then accelerate it independently of the music by 10-30% over the last act, or lock it to the cue's subdivision when the cue becomes rhythmic. The Ribicoff example supports rate modulation (speed up and slow down) tied to the story beat.
- INFERENCE, archival sound degradation rule: if source footage is degraded (low resolution, grain, damaged film), apply matching degradation to its procedural sound: low-pass around 3-5 kHz, high-pass around 100-200 Hz, slight saturation, mono, lower level (-6 to -12 dB relative to clean sound). Clean, vivid footage gets fuller-band sound. Derived from Ribicoff's "softened, smeared, quiet" practice; filter values are not from a source.
- INFERENCE, place-sourced score: when the catastrophe has a site (plant, factory, ship, city), prefer drones and pulses derived from field recordings or impulse responses of that kind of place (pitched-down machinery, pumps, sirens, radio static) over generic orchestral pads, as Chernobyl did.
- INFERENCE, suck-out: before the climactic hit, cut music and ambience to silence or near-silence (a -30 to -40 dB dip), optionally leaving a single high tinnitus-like sine (about 4-8 kHz, very low level) or room tone, for 0.5-1.5 s. See section 6 for the 500 ms minimum.

### Gaps
- No source found giving Dunkirk's tempo, the watch's tick rate, or Zimmer's own description of the Shepard implementation (CBC and other Zimmer interview pages were blocked).
- No primary source found on reversed sounds, tinnitus rings or radio static as documented techniques with numbers; they are listed as inferences only.
- Ken Burns' own stated sound philosophy (beyond Ribicoff's practice) was not retrieved; the Film Independent interview's full text was not fetched.

## 3. Composer and score examples and how their structure supports montage

### Takeaway
The cited scores share a low-register, slowly advancing threat (Sicario's drone and low percussion, Chernobyl's industrial textures), a choral or cluster "otherness" at a reveal (Ligeti's Requiem in Godzilla), and the braam as a punctuation device (Inception trailers). Details for Muse's "Isolated System" (World War Z), Arrival and trailer houses were not found.

### Cited Findings
- Sicario (Jóhann Jóhannsson, 2015): a 65-piece orchestra with emphasis on drums, strings and brass concentrated in the low register. — [Variety](https://variety.com/2015/artisans/production/sicario-score-johann-johannsson-1201594496/)
- Jóhannsson described the score as a beast lurching at you in slow motion; a recurring element in the theme is called "the drone". — [Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/awards/composers-johann-johannsson-sicario/5098958.article); [Edinburgh University Press 2025 article on Sicario, doi 10.3366/film.2025.0329](https://www.euppublishing.com/doi/10.3366/film.2025.0329)
- Villeneuve's brief for Sicario's music: "a threat, coming from under your feet". Jóhannsson called it subtle war music. — [Wikipedia: Sicario (soundtrack)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicario_(soundtrack)); [Gold Derby](https://www.goldderby.com/article/2015/sicario-johann-johannsson-emily-blunt-benicio-del-toro-oscar-predictions-13579086/); [IndieWire](https://www.indiewire.com/2016/02/how-they-made-the-percussive-sound-and-score-for-oscar-nominee-sicario-174951/)
- Godzilla (2014): Desplat's score echoes Ligeti's choral writing in cues like "Airport Attack" and "Golden Gate Chaos"; the film uses Ligeti's Requiem (not on the album) for the HALO-jump sequence into a darkened city, and a choral Ligeti reference closes the airport reveal. — [Wikipedia: Godzilla (2014 soundtrack)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godzilla_(2014_soundtrack)); [Filmtracks](https://filmtracks.com/titles/godzilla14.html); [Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2014/05/24/godzilla-alexandre-desplat/)
- Inception: Zimmer says the braam answered a screenplay call for massive, low musical tones; he later disliked that the score became a blueprint for action trailers. — [Wikipedia: BRAAAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRAAAM); [The Playlist](https://theplaylist.net/?p=92087)
- Chernobyl and Dunkirk: see section 2 (place-recorded industrial score; watch-tick pulse plus Shepard tone written into the script's structure).

### Inferences
- INFERENCE, a reusable "approaching catastrophe" cue template distilled from these examples: (1) low drone or sub pulse established first (Sicario), (2) a mechanical or ostinato pulse added (Dunkirk watch, string ostinato), (3) a continuous rising illusion underneath (Shepard-Risset), (4) a cluster/choral or braam event at the reveal (Godzilla/Ligeti, Inception), (5) cut to silence, then the hit. The montage cuts become denser at layers 2-3 and hold still at layer 4 so the reveal image gets time.
- INFERENCE: Ligeti-style micropolyphonic clusters (many voices on adjacent semitones, entering one by one) are a cheap procedural way to raise dissonance continuously without a melodic event; density (number of voices) can be driven by the montage timeline.

### Gaps
- Muse "Isolated System" in World War Z: nothing retrieved (tempo, ostinato structure, how the film cuts to it).
- Jóhannsson's Arrival score (vocal loops, low choir) not researched; note that Arrival's best-known opening/closing piece is Max Richter's "On the Nature of Daylight", not Jóhannsson.
- Two Steps From Hell and Audiomachine: no structural interview found.

## 4. Mixing: dynamic range, loudness targets, ducking, narration

### Takeaway
Impact comes from contrast: quiet before loud, with the hit following a planned silence. Cinema trailers are capped at 85 dB Leq(m) (TASA), which pushes mixers to win impact through contrast rather than sustained level. Streaming specs (Netflix) are dialogue-gated at -27 LKFS with -2 dBTP peaks, which preserves headroom for hits relative to narration. Documentary practice cuts picture during narration pauses and keeps archival sound low and softened.

### Cited Findings
- Cinema trailers: the maximum was first 87 dB Leq(m), lowered in two steps to 85 dB Leq(m), still in force for film and digital cinema; since 1999 the MPAA has not rated any trailer without TASA certification. — [ISDCF Doc11: Movie loudness measurement (2016)](https://files.isdcf.com/papers/ISDCF-Doc11-MovieLoudnessMeasurement20160315.pdf)
- Leq(m) uses M-weighting and is mainly used to ensure trailers respect cinema volume limits; it is not convertible to BS.1770 LKFS (different filter, 850 ms blocks, 1 kHz tone reference). — [Steinberg WaveLab reference](https://steinberg.help/wavelab_pro_plug_in_reference/v11/en/_shared/topics/plug_ref/supervision/supervision_modules_measurement_category_r.html); [Production Expert: Leq(m)](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/leq-m-loudness-what-is-it-and-when-is-it-used-iw6gp)
- Netflix: -27 LKFS dialogue-gated, -2 dBTP; tolerance reported as ±2 LU (2018 article) or ±0.5 LU (older v1.0 per iZotope). An older-spec dialogue LRA target of 7 LU or less is listed by iZotope. Check the current spec before use. — [Pro Tools Expert 2018](https://www.pro-tools-expert.com/home-page/2018/8/23/has-netflix-turned-the-clock-back-10-years-or-is-their-new-loudness-delivery-spec-a-stroke-of-genius); [iZotope Insight loudness docs](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/insight200/en/loudness/index.html); [The PostFlow delivery spec cheat sheet](https://thepostflow.com/filmmaker-resources/delivery-specs-cheat-sheet/)
- Silence makes a following sudden sound more frightening because it builds an aversive anticipatory state. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-composers-make-horror-movie-music-sound-terrifying/)
- Hits are typically followed by a long reverb tail; compress individual elements and limit the final mix. — [Ableton blog](https://www.ableton.com/en/blog/learn-how-to-make-high-impact-sounds-for-movies-and-trailers/)
- In The Vietnam War's Musgrave segment, the story is built around narration/testimony and picture editors cut in footage during his pauses; the heartbeat/helicopter bed rises and falls under it. — [MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/10/the-vietnam-war-sound-designer-on-enhancing-emotional-memories-of-the-war/)
- Nolan's concern on Dunkirk was how much is too much: sustained tension devices risk alienating the audience. — [The Playlist](https://theplaylist.net/nolan-hans-zimmer-dunkirk-score-20171130/)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE, level map for a narrated dread montage (dialogue-gated target around -24 to -27 LKFS for long-form, peak ceiling -2 dBTP): narration anchors at 0 dB reference; beds (drone, ostinato) under narration at -15 to -20 dB below narration; when narration pauses, beds come up to -6 to -10 dB below narration level within 200-400 ms; hits land in narration gaps only and peak 6-12 dB above the narration's short-term level, never on top of a spoken word.
- INFERENCE, ducking: sidechain duck beds by 6-10 dB with about 50-150 ms attack and 300-800 ms release on narration; do not duck hits, schedule them in gaps instead.
- INFERENCE, dynamic contrast: keep the integrated LRA of the montage in the 10-18 LU range for a cinematic feel (wider than a dialogue-only LRA of about 7 LU) and make the pre-hit dip at least 20-30 dB below the hit's peak short-term level. For small-speaker social delivery, narrow this (LRA about 6-10 LU), because quiet passages vanish on phones.
- INFERENCE, fatigue: limit full-scale hits to about 3-6 per minute and no more than one big hit per 8-10 s except in the final barrage; use the median of about 3 startles per horror trailer (section 6) as a ceiling for true jump-hits in a short piece.
- INFERENCE, narration policy: narration carries Acts 1-2 (setup and stakes); in Act 3 drop or thin narration to one short line in a gap, letting the cue and archival sound carry the climax; return a single line or a date/title card after the final hit.

### Gaps
- No source found giving trailer-house numbers for how loud hits sit relative to dialogue, or measured LRA of trailers.
- YouTube/social platform normalisation targets (commonly said to be around -14 LUFS integrated) were not verified in this session.
- Current Netflix spec version (tolerance, LRA guidance) not fetched directly.

## 5. Picture-sound synchronisation rules

### Takeaway
Sources agree on hits on cuts and planned silence before the hit, and on not cutting on every beat. No retrieved source gives numeric J-cut leads or flash-frame lengths; those values are inferences, and the main hard numbers come from the startle literature in section 6.

### Cited Findings
- Transitions land on musical hits; the title card sits on the loudest moment after a beat of silence. — [pexo.ai](https://pexo.ai/tutorial/how-to-make-a-movie-trailer); [Flashboards](https://flashboards.yaroflasher.com/learn/editing/movie-trailer/)
- Use strong beats for reveals and title cards; cutting on every beat becomes robotic; match some cuts to on-screen motion instead. — [Epikton](https://epikton.net/a-quick-guide-to-pacing-in-trailers/); [Derek Lieu](https://www.derek-lieu.com/blog/2018/1/6/what-im-thinking-when-i-edit-a-trailer)
- Silence needs to be planned at the picture-editing level. — [Designing Sound, Bryan Jerden](https://designingsound.org/2012/07/19/exclusive-interview-bryan-jerden-on-trailer-sound-design/)
- Whooshes are sized to the movement on screen (short or long). — [Ableton blog](https://www.ableton.com/en/blog/learn-how-to-make-high-impact-sounds-for-movies-and-trailers/)
- Braams are used with CGI collisions and fades to black. — [Wikipedia: BRAAAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRAAAM)
- Documentary rule: footage is cut into the pauses of testimony/narration. — [MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/10/the-vietnam-war-sound-designer-on-enhancing-emotional-memories-of-the-war/)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE, hit-cut alignment: place the hard cut on the hit's transient frame (frame-exact). If a frame is ambiguous at 24/25/30 fps, put the cut 0-1 frame after the transient rather than before, since audio arriving slightly after the picture is tolerated better than audio arriving early (see Gaps on ITU figures).
- INFERENCE, J-cuts: lead the next scene's ambience or archival sound by about 8-24 frames (0.3-1.0 s) into the previous shot for scene changes inside an act; lead by 1-2 beats at phase transitions (Act 1 to 2) so the sound announces the new section before the picture.
- INFERENCE, flash frames: on the 1-3 biggest hits, use 1-3 frames of white or of the next image (or an overexposed archival frame) exactly on the transient, then the held image. Avoid repeated flashes faster than about 3 per second for photosensitivity safety (common broadcast guidance; not verified here).
- INFERENCE, syncopation: in Act 2, place some cuts on the "and" (8th-note offbeat) or on the last 16th before a downbeat so the ear expects the downbeat and the eye arrives early; resolve to strict downbeat cuts in Act 3 for the sense of something locking in. This is an editorial heuristic, not a measured effect.
- INFERENCE, riser-to-hit: end a riser exactly at the suck-out start, not at the hit; hold silence 0.5-1.5 s; then hit + cut together. This combines "riser then brief silence" (Ableton) with the startle timing in section 6.

### Gaps
- No source found giving frame counts for J-cut leads, flash-frame lengths, or measured syncopation effects in trailers.
- ITU-R BT.1359 audio/video sync thresholds (commonly cited as detectable at about 45 ms audio-early and 125 ms audio-late) were not verified in this session.
- No trailer-editor interview (Trailer Park, Mark Woollen) with explicit sync numbers was retrieved.

## 6. Psychoacoustic evidence (startle, prepulse inhibition, infrasound, unpredictability)

### Takeaway
Startle is strongest after quiet and anticipation, and is suppressed if a weaker sound precedes it by about 30-500 ms (prepulse inhibition); beyond about 500 ms the lead can facilitate it instead. Infrasound (about 17 Hz) has tentative evidence of unease even unnoticed, but consumer playback cannot reproduce it. Rough, nonlinear sounds and unpredictable timing increase fear. Horror trailers use a median of about 3 acoustic startles each.

### Cited Findings
- Prepulse inhibition: the acoustic startle response is reduced by a weaker prepulse 30-500 ms earlier; common lead intervals are 30, 60, 120, 240, 480 ms; with leads over 500 ms, prepulse facilitation (a larger response) is more likely; prepulses are typically 3-12 dB above background (65-70 dB in human studies). — [Wikipedia: Prepulse inhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prepulse_inhibition); [Orbilu abstract (Blumenthal co-author, 2015)](https://orbilu.uni.lu/handle/10993/49915)
- Conflicting data: a 2018 human study varying 30-1000 ms found inhibition at 60, 120 and 250 ms but no effect at 30 and 500 ms. — [Journal of Clinical and Diagnostic Research 2018](https://www.jcdr.net/articles/PDF/11411/36016_CE(RA1)_F(AP)_PF1(MJ_AP)_PFA(MJ_AP)_PB(MJ_AP)_PN(AP).pdf)
- Anticipation increases startle magnitude (Grillon et al. 1993) and reported fright (Cantor et al. 1984); only 3 of 18 startle studies reviewed by Martin (2019) used audiovisual stimuli. — [Acosta & Sears, "(Re)Considering the jump scare in four elements", Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1569394/full)
- Horror audio manipulates fear two ways: slow phasic suspense and startle. Analysis of acoustic startle events (ASEs): 173 ASEs in the sample, 1 to 10 per item, median 3. (The search summary says per trailer; the paper title refers to horror films; verify the unit.) — [Sbravatti, "Acoustic Startles in Horror Films: A Neurofilmological Approach" (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332534339_Acoustic_Startles_in_Horror_Films_A_Neurofilmological_Approach); [Psychology and neurobiology of horror movies (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368024569_Psychology_and_neurobiology_of_horror_movies)
- Lab practice for unpredictability: startle probes placed at varying intervals of 20-90 s after clip onset. — [PMC: laterality in emotion elicited by film clips](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3003668/)
- Infrasound: the 2003 "Infrasonic" concerts (Sarah Angliss, Richard Wiseman, Richard Lord of the UK National Physical Laboratory) added about 17 Hz sine waves to parts of live music; questionnaires gave tentative evidence of unease even when listeners were unaware; 22% reported more unusual experiences (unease, sorrow, chills, revulsion or fear) when infrasound was present. Venue and pipe-length details differ between reports. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna3077192); [Al Jazeera 2003](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2003/9/8/sound-of-silence-gives-ghoul-vibrations); [Wikipedia: Sarah Angliss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Angliss)
- Rough, nonlinear sounds (like screams and alarm calls) signal danger; horror scores mimic them (Blumstein et al., 102 film scores); Psycho's high strings imitate scream roughness. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-composers-make-horror-movie-music-sound-terrifying/); [CBC Music: the sound of fear](https://www.cbc.ca/music/sound-of-fear-adriana-barton-scary-music-science-1.7005107)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE, hard rule for maximum hit impact: the 30-500 ms window before a hit must contain no transient louder than the background (no tick, no drum, no whoosh tail ending inside it, no word onset). Either keep total silence/room tone for at least 500-800 ms before the hit, or use a riser that ends at least 500 ms before the hit (lead > 500 ms tends to facilitate). Whooshes that crash straight into the hit work for energy but by this evidence soften the startle; choose per hit.
- INFERENCE, deliberate softening: for the many smaller accents, a soft pre-hit (a reverse swell or tick 60-250 ms before) is a tool to reduce startle and fatigue while keeping rhythm.
- INFERENCE, unpredictability: in Act 1-2 vary the gap between big events (for example jitter of ±1-2 beats around the expected phrase boundary) so that viewers cannot predict them; in Act 3 switch to strict regularity, so dread turns into inevitability.
- INFERENCE, low frequency for real devices: true infrasound (<20 Hz) will not play on phones, laptops or most TVs and may be filtered by codecs. Use 30-60 Hz drones with strong 2nd-4th harmonics (60-240 Hz) so the pitch is implied on small speakers, and slow amplitude modulation (0.5-4 Hz) for a "pressure" feeling. Treat the 17 Hz evidence as tentative and only relevant for full-range playback.
- INFERENCE, roughness: add amplitude or frequency modulation in the roughly 30-80 Hz range, or slight detuning between layers, to drones and strings at the peak of tension to approximate the rough, scream-like quality horror scores borrow; keep it off during narration.
- INFERENCE, startle budget: about 3 true startles per short piece (median of the horror sample), placed after the longest quiet sections.

### Gaps
- The Blumenthal et al. (2005) startle guidelines (Psychophysiology) were not retrieved; the 30-500 ms window rests on secondary sources plus one conflicting primary study.
- No study found that tests prepulse inhibition directly with film or trailer audio.
- No peer-reviewed replication of the 2003 infrasound concert results was found; reports conflict on venue and apparatus.
- No numeric source found on optimal dB contrast between pre-hit quiet and the hit for audiences (lab startle stimuli are usually around 95-105 dB, but this was not verified here).
