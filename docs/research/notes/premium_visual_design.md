# Premium maps, 3D and motion graphics in historical documentaries: what makes them look prestige-level, and how it is produced

Research date: 2026-10-27. About 37 tool calls. Some pages could not be fetched: Blender manual and Blender wiki returned 403, the Netflix partner pages redirect to a login-gated hub, Behance returned 429, and the Duncan Elms project page returned 404. Where a finding comes from a search-engine excerpt rather than a page I fetched in full, it is marked "(search excerpt)". Treat those as less certain.

## 1. Case studies: named productions and studios, and what they did

### Takeaway
The best-documented recent prestige case studies are Netflix's *Turning Point: The Bomb and the Cold War* (2024, graphics by Antenna) and *World War II: From the Frontlines* (2023, graphics by Elastic, Emmy for graphic design). They share a pattern: one series-wide design system (same colours, textures, fonts, pacing and camera behaviour in every episode), maps that carry changing borders and alliances, archival textures from scanned documents composited in 3D space, and infographics built storyboard-first. I could not find who made the 3D maps for Ken Burns and Lynn Novick's *The Vietnam War*.

### Inferences
- What reads as "Hollywood" in these credits is not one effect. It is a single system applied with discipline across hours of material. Antenna explicitly locked colour, texture, fonts, pacing, camera movement and effect attributes. In an automated pipeline, the equivalent is one style/token file that every map, title and infographic generator must read. No per-shot freestyling.
- Elastic's treatment of stills shows a repeatable "premium" recipe that a browser-plus-Blender pipeline can copy: scanned paper or document textures, composited over archival stills, placed on planes in 3D space with a slow camera move.
- The Nat Geo work shows the "archive-to-present alignment" device: camera-project an archival aerial photo onto modern terrain, then crossfade. Blender can do this with camera projection (UV Project modifier, or a projected texture from a camera matching the archive frame).
- The prestige productions mix practical elements (ice, paper, document scans) with CG. That tactile layer is a large part of why they do not look like templates.
- *Why We Fight* set the conventions that still dominate war maps: territory as area fill, movement as arrows. The NECSUS caution matters for a documentary pipeline: animated fills and arrows persuade. The style should signal uncertainty, for example with dashed or faded edges for contested areas, rather than imply false precision.

## 2. Visual language of premium maps: palette, typography, hierarchy, relief, textures, fronts and blocs

### Takeaway
Premium maps follow classical cartographic rules, then add restraint and texture:
- a strict label hierarchy, with at most one serif and one sans family;
- water labels in blue italic, area labels in letter-spaced capitals;
- relief lit from the north-west or north-north-west, with warm highlights, blue-grey shadows and atmospheric fading of the lowlands;
- a muted base so that only the narrative actors carry colour.

Territory is shown as area fill and movement as arrows, the convention since *Why We Fight*. Changing borders and alliances are animated as part of a consistent series system.

### Cited Findings

**Typography and label hierarchy (Axis Maps, *Cartography Guide*)**
- Hierarchy. "Bigger and bolder styles tend to promote labels"; capitals and strong colours (black, red, purple) also promote. Smaller sizes, tighter tracking and muted greys demote. Ranked categories should differ by at least two points when sizes are small. — [Axis Maps: Labeling](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/labeling)
- Use serif for physical features and sans serif for cultural features, or the reverse; the point is to distinguish the two classes. On screen at small sizes, sans serif is usually safest. If serifs are used on screen, heavier, block-like or square serifs work best. — [Axis Maps](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/labeling)
- Water features are labelled in blue italics. Area features are labelled in uppercase, visually centred and letter-spaced to show their extent. — [Axis Maps](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/labeling)
- Point-label position priority: above-right first, then below-right, above-left, below-left. Directly above, below or to the side is not preferred. Never rotate labels upside-down. — [Axis Maps](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/labeling)
- At most one serif and one sans serif family, and never more than one sans. Minimum size is about 9–10 pt for on-screen maps (6–7 pt for print). Point size alone does not decide legibility. — [Axis Maps](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/labeling)
- Hierarchy is needed because "there are too many labels to show in too small of a space". — [Penn State, Text on Maps](https://courses.ems.psu.edu/maps/node/1732) (search excerpt)
- The cartographer must change typographic visual variables to support both the hierarchy and the link between each label and its feature. — [UCGIS GIS&T Body of Knowledge](https://gistbok.ucgis.org/node/100001358) (search excerpt)

**Relief and terrain styling (the Imhof / Swiss tradition, and modern Blender practice)**
- Imhof's aerial-perspective principle applies to both monochrome and full-colour relief. For coloured shaded relief he suggests grey-blue or blue-violet-grey shadows. — [Cartographic Perspectives (Tóth)](https://cartographicperspectives.org/index.php/journal/article/download/cp67-toth/pdf/) (search excerpt)
- Imhof "feathered a sense of atmospheric perspective into his hillshades". — [Esri ArcGIS blog](https://esri.com/arcgis-blog/?p=310032) (search excerpt)
- Aerial perspective is used deliberately to separate high summits from lower, more distant lowlands. — [Cartographica, relief shading paper](https://carto.geogr.msu.ru/cmc/publications/papers/cartographica/carto_38-1_8_reliefshading.pdf) (search excerpt)
- Daniel Huffman's terrain technique: a brighter, warmer tone on the lit side of high mountains, and a blue tint in the shadows. — [Axis Maps blog: Relief mapping in 10 easy steps](https://axismaps.com/blog/relief-mapping-in-10-easy-steps) (search excerpt)
- The Axis Maps workflow: take a good DEM (from the USGS National Map), render shaded relief in Blender, then combine it with land cover in Photoshop using Multiply. — [Axis Maps blog](https://axismaps.com/blog/relief-mapping-in-10-easy-steps) (search excerpt)
- Cartographers are increasingly rendering shaded relief in Blender. — [ICA Proceedings 2021](https://ica-proc.copernicus.org/articles/4/111/2021/ica-proc-4-111-2021.pdf) (search excerpt); [Andy Woodruff, Seasonal relief](https://andywoodruff.com/blog/seasonal-relief/) (search excerpt)
- **Huffman's Blender relief settings** — [Daniel Huffman, Creating shaded relief in Blender](https://somethingaboutmaps.wordpress.com/2017/11/16/creating-shaded-relief-in-blender/) (version history and reader Q&A; the main chapter text was not in the fetched excerpt):
  - Heightmap. A 16-bit DEM gives about 65,000 elevation steps versus 256 at 8-bit, so "things will look smooth". The heightmap texture must use the Non-Color colour space.
  - Elevation range. Rescale elevations into the 0–65,535 range. Offset negative values so the minimum is 0. Fill NoData with black, not white, so it casts no shadows.
  - Resolution and smoothing. A rule of thumb is a DEM of 100–150 dpi. Smooth an over-detailed DEM, for example with a Gaussian blur, so the major structures read clearly.
  - Displacement. Set the material's displacement to "Displacement Only", with Cycles and experimental features enabled. Use adaptive subdivision rather than full mesh subdivision.
  - Sampling. "200-ish" samples is enough for most uses. Raise it to reduce noise at shadow edges.
  - Large renders. Split them into chunks, with overlap so that shadows do not stop abruptly at chunk edges.
  - Exaggeration and light. Huffman advises against very high vertical exaggeration. A broader sun angle softens stepping artefacts from 8-bit heightmaps.
- **Light direction.** The cartographic convention puts the light source at the north-west to avoid relief inversion, where valleys read as ridges. Biland & Çöltekin (2017) found that north-north-west (337.5°), or even north, works better. — [Biland & Çöltekin 2017 (PDF)](https://intranet.inegi.org.mx/calidad/doc/2019/Indicadores_CyE/Guia_didactica/2017_Biland-NNW_CAGIS.pdf) (search excerpt)
- A 2018 replication with a South African sample broadly confirmed that NNW (or N) beats NW. — [ISPRS Archives 2018](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLII-4/101/2018/) (search excerpt)
- A 2025 abstract reports the strongest inversion illusion at azimuth 225° (light from the south-west). It tests multidirectional lighting as a way to reduce the effect. — [ICA Abstracts 2025](https://ica-abs.copernicus.org/articles/10/92/2025/ica-abs-10-92-2025.pdf) (search excerpt)
- Tom Patterson's *Prisma Shaded Relief* was rendered with multiple light sources, then artistically filtered and mosaicked. — [Natural Earth](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/?p=5025) (search excerpt)

**Textures, fronts, movements and blocs**
- Elastic overlaid historical textures from scanned documents onto stills and footage in 3D space. — [Behance](https://www.behance.net/gallery/193582217/World-War-II-From-The-Frontlines-Netflix) (search excerpt)
- Antenna's maps carried "ever-changing borders", weapons capabilities and historical alliances. Final passes added "colour and texture" after timing had been locked. — [Antenna](https://antennacreative.com/projects/turningpoint)
- Bloc convention since WWII: Axis-occupied territory as solid black fill, and arrows on global projections for army movements. — [Wikipedia: Why We Fight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_We_Fight); [DVD Talk](https://www.dvdtalk.com/reviews/review/73588) (search excerpts)
- Clients commissioning documentary battle maps expect moving arrows for the battle plan, enemy and defensive positions, smoke puffs and battle symbols. — [job brief via search](https://flexionis.wuaze.com/job/motion-graphics-designer-historical-map-animation-documentary) (search excerpt; low-authority source, conventions only)

### Inferences
These are proposed pipeline rules, not sourced facts.
- **Palette.** Use a low-chroma base: desaturated terrain, ocean a few steps darker than land, borders thin and grey. Give saturated colour only to the two or three actors the narration is talking about, and keep each actor's colour identical across the series, as Antenna did. Blocs are shown with area fills at partial opacity over relief, so the terrain stays visible.
- **Typography.** Use one serif family for physical geography (seas, rivers, ranges; water in blue italic) and one sans family for political and cultural labels (countries in letter-spaced capitals, cities in title case with a dot). No more than three size steps visible at once. At 1080p, treat Axis Maps' 9–10 pt screen minimum as roughly 22–28 px cap-height. That pixel conversion is my estimate.
- **Relief.** Use a sun azimuth of about 315–337.5° and never near 225°. Add warm highlights and blue-grey shadows, and fade the lowlands toward the haze colour (Imhof aerial perspective). In Blender this is a Mist or Z pass mixed toward a haze colour in the compositor, or a Volume Scatter domain. Keep vertical exaggeration moderate: Huffman warns against high values, and I found no published number.
- **Fronts and movements.**
  - Area fill means control or occupation.
  - A drawn-on line, the "trace", means a front or a route.
  - A tapered arrow means intent or an advance.
  - A dashed or faded edge means contested or uncertain.

  Draw arrows on progressively along a spline, with a trailing taper, rather than sliding pre-made arrows. Width can encode force size, as in a Minard-style flow, when the data supports it.
- **Texture.** Composite a paper or scanned-document texture in Multiply or Overlay at low opacity over both the map and the archival stills, so CG and archive share one surface.

- No primary source found on Imhof-style hypsometric tint values for screen use, or on recommended vertical exaggeration numbers.
- Line-label placement rules (rivers, borders) and halo use are not covered in the Axis Maps excerpt.

## 3. Rendering and finishing: colour management, lighting, atmosphere, grain, halation, matching CG to archive

### Takeaway
- **Colour.** For Blender CG, use AgX, the default since Blender 4.0, for photographic terrain. Use Khronos PBR Neutral (Blender 4.2+) or flat 2D compositing for graphic colours whose hue must not shift.
- **Atmosphere.** Fade distance toward haze in the compositor.
- **Unifying finish.** One film-emulation layer over CG and archive alike: sampled grain, subtle halation, gate weave matched to the era of the footage.
- **Data hygiene.** Deliver linear EXR internally (Netflix rejects log EXRs in VFX delivery), and keep colour references with the frames.

### Cited Findings

**Colour management**
- Blender 4.0 release notes: the AgX view transform "replaces Filmic as the default in new files". It handles overexposed areas better, with bright colours going toward white, "similar to real cameras". — [Blender 4.0 release notes: Color Management](https://wiki.blender.org/release_notes/4.0/color_management/) (search excerpt; the page returned 403 when fetched)
- AgX was positioned as avoiding the hue shifting of ACES and the "notorious 6" problem of Filmic. — [BlenderNation: Filmic's successor AgX in Blender 4.0](https://www.blendernation.com/2023/09/05/filmics-successor-new-agx-view-transform-in-blender-4-0/) (search excerpt)
- Criticism: AgX defaults can look a bit dull, and some colours such as neon yellow are hard to reach. The author recommends a "raw" workflow for vivid colours. — [CG Cookie: The Secret to Rendering Vibrant Colors with AgX](https://cgcookie.com/comments/387969/replies/new) (search excerpt)
- One user's measurements: indigo shifted from 243° under Filmic to 232° under AgX, and brass from 52° to 46°. Some replies call AgX non-neutral, others say the shift is an intended part of the look. Khronos PBR Neutral, added in Blender 4.2, is designed to keep hues predictable. — [Blender Artists: AgX changes hue](https://blenderartists.org/t/agx-changes-hue/1527123) (search excerpt; single-user test)
- AgX reference implementation by its author. — [GitHub EaryChow/AgX](https://github.com/EaryChow/AgX)

**Netflix delivery and pipeline**
- Netflix VFX Best Practices: VFX pulls "should always include dailies color reference (QuickTime or reference frames) along with any CDLs and/or LUTs". — [Netflix VFX Best Practices](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000611467-VFX-Best-Practices) (search excerpt; now redirects to a gated "Knowledge Hub")
- Netflix's Situational VFX Delivery Specifications state that LOG EXRs will not be accepted. — [Netflix Situational VFX Delivery Specifications](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/21668717059475-Situational-VFX-Delivery-Specifications) (search excerpt)
- Third-party EXR guidance: PIZ or ZIP (lossless) compression for final or archive, DWAA for sharing work-in-progress. — [fast.io EXR guide](https://fast.io/resources/exr-files.md) (search excerpt; not Netflix)

**Grain, halation, gate weave and matching CG to archive**
- Grain extraction. Divide the archival plate by a blurred copy of itself to isolate the grain, then overlay that grain on the CG. Render or apply grain against mid-grey rather than white to avoid tinting. — [Blender Artists: matching grain of real footage and CG](https://blenderartists.org/t/how-to-match-grain-noise-of-real-footage-and-cg-stuff/542813) (search excerpt)
- For documentaries, rougher 16 mm or 8 mm grain overlays can unify disparate sources. Grain should "interact with your image, not sit on top of it". — [HolyGrain: grain overlays by genre](https://www.holygrain.com/blog/best-film-grain-overlays-for-different-genres/) (search excerpt)
- Grain as image sequences suits CG, roto, stabilisation, retiming and colour-managed EXR pipelines. — [HolyGrain: best grain format](https://www.holygrain.com/blog/best-film-grain-format-for-video/) (search excerpt)
- Grain alone does not read as film. It needs grade, contrast, halation and gate weave together. — [No Film School: four elements of film emulation](https://nofilmschool.com/four-elements-film-emulation) (search excerpt)
- Filmbox models halation to match a negative's "soft yet detailed character". Its gate weave control has adjustable intensity, to match different cameras and gauges. — [Video Village: Filmbox](https://videovillage.com/filmbox/) (search excerpt)
- It is easier to add halation and grain to digital images than to remove them from film. — [Wikipedia: Film emulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_emulation) (search excerpt)
- **Archival handling:**
  - pillarbox 4:3 material rather than cropping it (cropping loses the composition and doubles the apparent grain);
  - retime silent footage shot at 16–18 fps, which plays too fast at 24 fps;
  - restore only as much damage as the story needs.
  — [Flashboards archival footage guide](https://flashboards.yaroflasher.com/learn/editing/archival-footage/) (search excerpt; secondary source)
- MPC's virtual cinematography on *Prehistoric Planet* aimed for "the most authentic documentary-style aesthetic". — [fxguide: Prehistoric Planet](https://www.fxguide.com/?p=285876) (search excerpt)

### Inferences
These are pipeline proposals, not sourced facts.
- **Render passes and compositing.** Render terrain in Blender (AgX, base contrast) to linear EXR with these passes: beauty, Mist/Z, AO, shadow, and a Cryptomatte or object-index mask for each bloc region. Do the following in the compositor or browser layer, not baked into the render:
  - haze: Mist mixed toward a cool, desaturated haze colour, stronger with distance;
  - a subtle bloom on highlights only;
  - the grade;
  - grain.

  That way one render serves several looks.
- **Graphic colours.** Bloc fills, arrows and labels should be composited as display-referred 2D layers after tone mapping, in the browser. Otherwise AgX's highlight desaturation and hue skew will change the brand colours, as the Blender Artists measurements show. If fills must sit in 3D, render them with Standard or Khronos PBR Neutral.
- **Lighting.** Low sun at azimuth 315–337.5° (Section 2), 15–30° elevation for long modelling shadows, a cool sky/fill colour, and optionally a faint rim from behind the camera's line to separate ridges from haze. The elevation range is my judgement; no source gives one.
- **One finishing layer for everything.** Apply the same film-emulation stack over CG maps, archive and titles:
  - grain sampled from the dominant archival source;
  - halation of a few pixels on bright edges only;
  - gate weave of at most about 1 px, matched to the archive's era;
  - a gentle softening, because pin-sharp CG next to 16 mm archive reads as fake.

  Unless the shot is meant as an archive pastiche, keep chromatic aberration and lens distortion to barely perceptible levels, or skip them.
- **Archive alignment shot.** Camera-project archival aerial stills onto DEM terrain in Blender, the Nat Geo device, then crossfade to the modern texture.

## 4. Camera language for CG in documentaries

### Takeaway
Prestige documentary CG constrains the virtual camera to what a real crew could have shot: long lenses, a limited number of positions, drone or helicopter-style moves, and positions chosen from on-the-ground logistics. That is the main defence against the "video game" look. In graphics-led series, camera pacing and movement are standardised across all shots, as Antenna did.

### Cited Findings
- *Prehistoric Planet* (Apple TV+; MPC; BBC Studios Natural History Unit). Co-director Mike Gunton said the VFX team, used to placing a camera anywhere, had to accept documentary limits:
  - a long lens for a sequence;
  - not getting the camera close;
  - only one camera position;
  - cutting away to a drone.

  The approach was to back out the constraints and lucky moments of real nature filming, so the result looks like found footage. — [AWN: Prehistoric Planet season 2](https://awn.com/vfxworld/prehistoric-planet-season-2-takes-dino-storytelling-extreme); [IBC: Behind the scenes, Prehistoric Planet II](https://ibc.org/features/behind-the-scenes-prehistoric-planet-ii/9645.article); [befores & afters](https://beforesandafters.com/2023/07/26/cardboard-cut-outs-were-our-mainstay/) (search excerpts; the summary does not say which of these three pages carries which quote)
- MPC's Elliot Newman: camera position and movement were chosen from the actual logistics of the scene, as if the crew were physically present. Directors scouted CG scenes in VR, with guidance from a BBC wildlife crew expert on how such behaviour would really be filmed. — same sources as above (search excerpts); [befores & afters 2022](https://beforesandafters.com/2022/06/08/those-close-ups-are-where-it-all-lives-and-breathes/)
- Previs was used both to brief location camera teams (Iceland, Saudi Arabia, Zambia) and to plan virtual cameras at MPC London and LA. — [fxguide](https://www.fxguide.com/?p=285876); [IBC](https://ibc.org/features/behind-the-scenes-prehistoric-planet-ii/9645.article) (search excerpts)
- Antenna kept "pacing and camera movement" similar from shot to shot across the series. For the ice title, camera movement was tuned to the material and refined through tests. — [Antenna](https://antennacreative.com/projects/turningpoint)
- Elastic animated stills and document textures "in 3D space", which gives parallax to flat archive. — [Behance](https://www.behance.net/gallery/193582217/World-War-II-From-The-Frontlines-Netflix) (search excerpt)
- Nat Geo *Secrets From Above* used camera projection to move through archival aerial photos. — [Mattmograph](https://mattmograph.com/portfolio/national-geographic:-wwii:-secrets-from-above-documentary)
- The Ken Burns effect, slow moves across still photographs, is the root documentary camera grammar for stills. — [Realscreen](https://realscreen.com/2017/02/17/ken-burns-blends-alchemy-and-archive-in-the-vietnam-war/) (search excerpt)

### Inferences
These are pipeline rules, not sourced facts.
- **Virtual rig.** Model the camera as a physical aircraft or drone or crane: no roll except a gentle bank on turns, a bounded speed, and every path a smooth spline. For oblique map shots, prefer long equivalent focal lengths (about 50–135 mm on a full-frame sensor) to compress terrain and look like aerial photography. Use wide lenses only for deliberate low "over the ridge" reveals. This focal-length range is my recommendation and is not sourced.
- **One move per shot.** A single motivated move — a push toward the place the narrator names, a lateral track for parallax, or a slow tilt-down reveal — with long ease-in/ease-out and no direction changes mid-shot. The move starts before the line that motivates it and settles before labels appear.
- **Parallax.** Use parallax from layered depth (terrain, haze, cloud cards, label plane), not from fast motion. Shallow depth of field should be rare and subtle on maps. Strong tilt-shift blur reads as a "miniature" gimmick.
- **Consistency.** Fix speed and easing presets per series, as Antenna's "pacing and camera movement" consistency suggests. Store them in the style file so generated shots cannot drift.

- No interview found on how *The Vietnam War*'s 3D maps were shot. The camera style is unknown.

## 5. Motion design principles for documentary infographics, and how long text must hold

### Takeaway
Text should stay on screen long enough to be read twice at a slow reading pace (about 200 wpm), roughly 0.6 s per word. It should never run faster than subtitle reading-speed limits (Netflix: 20 characters per second for adults). Timing and storyboard come first, and colour and texture are added last (Antenna's process). The classical principles of easing, anticipation, overshoot and staging carry over to documentary graphics, but overshoot should be used sparingly in a sober historical register.

### Cited Findings
- **Netflix English Timed Text Style Guide:**
  - reading speed up to 20 characters per second for adult programmes, 17 for children's;
  - 42 characters per line, maximum 2 lines, with a bottom-heavy pyramid preferred when breaking;
  - forced narratives (translations of on-screen text) should match the on-screen text's duration where possible, and are set in ALL CAPS except long prologues and epilogues.
  — [Netflix Partner Help: English Timed Text Style Guide](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)
- "Leave text on screen for at least as long as it takes to read through twice." — [UC Davis Communications Guide: Text on screen](https://communicationsguide.ucdavis.edu/departments/video/best-practices/accessibility/text-on-screen) (search excerpt)
- The same "read it twice" benchmark, assuming slow readers at about 200 words per minute. Complex text needs more time, and script fonts or busy backgrounds need a longer hold. — [SSW Rules: Do you give enough time to read texts?](https://www.ssw.com.au/rules/post-production-do-you-give-enough-time-to-read-texts-in-your-videos) (search excerpt)
- About 1.5–3 s for short on-screen text, and about 3–6 s for names and lower thirds. — [Overseeros blog](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/how-long-should-on-screen-text-stay-youtube) (search excerpt; secondary source)
- Title formula: about 3 s for a ten-letter title, plus 1 s for every five additional letters. — [Pinnacle Studio help](https://help.pinnaclesys.com/pinnacle/v24/en/help/pinnacle-studio/h2-giving-your-project-a-title.html) (search excerpt)
- Antenna's infographic process went storyboard (concept and timing), then refinement, then final colour and texture. — [Antenna](https://antennacreative.com/projects/turningpoint)
- Easing terminology flips between tools. After Effects and traditional animation call a slow start "ease-out", while CSS and JS call the same behaviour "ease-in". Anticipation and overshoot are taught as a pair, ending with overshoot. — [Universität für angewandte Kunst Wien, Motion Design 01](https://computerstudio.uni-ak.ac.at/tutorials/motion-design-01/) (search excerpt)
- The 12 principles come from Thomas and Johnston's *The Illusion of Life* and apply to 3D work as well. — [University of Washington CSE 458: Animation Principles](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse458/20au/content/exercises/animation_principles.html) (search excerpt)
- Anticipation prepares the audience for an action, for example a character looking off-screen before someone arrives. — [Pluralsight: 12 Principles of Animation](https://www.pluralsight.com/resources/blog/software-development/understanding-12-principles-animation) (search excerpt)

### Inferences
These are pipeline rules, not sourced facts.
- **Hold-time formula for an automated checker:**
  - `hold_seconds = max(1.5, 0.6 × words, chars / 20)`, measured from fully legible to the start of the exit.
  - Add +0.5 s for serif or italic map labels, or labels over busy terrain.
  - Add +1 s when the narrator is speaking different content at the same time.
  - In/out animations do not count toward the hold.

  The figures come from: two reads at 200 wpm ≈ 0.6 s/word (UC Davis / SSW), and Netflix's 20 cps as an absolute floor.
- **Sequencing on maps.** Introduce one new idea per beat, for example camera settles → region fill → label → arrow. Stagger entries by 6–12 frames rather than revealing everything at once. Labels should arrive after the move settles, not during it. This is "staging" applied to infographics.
- **Easing register.** For historical documentaries, use slow, long ease-in-out curves, and minimal or no overshoot or bounce on maps and data. Keep overshoot for small UI-like accents if at all. Overshoot and squash read as playful or "explainer" style, which conflicts with a prestige register.
- **Process order for the generator:** script line → timing and storyboard (animatic with grey boxes) → motion lock → colour and texture pass → finishing (grain, halation). This mirrors Antenna's process, and it lets timing be checked before expensive renders.

### Gaps
- No primary source from a prestige documentary studio gives hold times, stagger intervals or easing presets. The hold-time sources are general video and accessibility guides and Netflix subtitle rules, not documentary graphics guidelines.
- No source found that specifically discusses avoiding overshoot or "bouncy" motion in serious documentary graphics. That recommendation is my inference.
