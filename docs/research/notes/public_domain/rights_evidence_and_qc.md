# Rights evidence and quality control for public-repository archival footage (as of 2026-10; not legal advice)

Scope: how documentary producers and clearance reviewers verify and document public-domain or licensed status of archival footage, and what an automated editing system should record per clip so a finished film can pass clearance review. Researched 2026-10-27. Sources are mostly US (NARA, 17 U.S.C. §105, US E&O practice). Several industry documents (APA guideline PDFs, insurer clearance-procedure attachments, sample clearance logs) could not be retrieved in full; those are listed under Gaps. Items under "Inferences" are my reasoning, not sourced facts.

## 1. What archival producers and clearance attorneys require (chain of title, licences, catalog records, §105 determinations and their limits, clearance-log formats)

### Takeaway
Clearance review runs on a rights and clearances log that lists every rights-contingent item. An attorney reads that log, and anything left off it is not covered by the attorney's letter or the E&O policy. For US-government footage, the repository will not certify status. NARA states that it does not confirm copyright status, indemnify users, or license content, so the producer has to assemble the evidence: a §105 basis, catalog records, deed or donor terms, and checks for embedded third-party material. §105 covers only work by federal employees within their official duties. Contractor and grantee works, off-duty employee works and embedded third-party material all fall outside it.

### Cited Findings
**The log and who reviews it**
- A university production handbook says the first step toward E&O insurance is a rights and clearances log covering all rights-contingent material ("still photographs, artwork, sourced audio clips or audiovisual media clips, commissioned score"). "The log is usually reviewed by an attorney who drafts a risk assessment letter based on this log." It also warns: "Any instance not listed in your log will not be covered under the attorney letter or any issued insurance policy." — [Occidental College production handbook, "Producing Rights"](https://www.oxy.edu/media-arts-culture/student-resources/production-handbook/shooting-your-project/producing-rights)
- The same handbook says incidental material, such as background music, must be logged too: "Logging an instance does not mean you will necessarily ever need to pay rights or even contact a rights holder." — [Occidental College](https://www.oxy.edu/media-arts-culture/student-resources/production-handbook/shooting-your-project/producing-rights)
- One IDA practitioner account describes a clip log, used for attorney review, that recorded copyright holder, licensing status and the intended fair-use claim for each clip. — [Rawady, "Clip Show: A Practical Guide to Fair Use," IDA, 2009](https://documentary.org/node/34395)

**Insurer-style clearance procedures (chain of title, licences, releases)**
- Clearance should start at the film's inception, continue through filming and finish at final cut. A copyright report should confirm "the chain of title of all works on which the production is based is thoroughly investigated." For clips and photographs, "all licenses and consents must be obtained from the copyright owner" and from people depicted. Written releases are needed for "faces and likenesses of any recognizable living persons." Music must be cleared with composers and performers and with owners of pre-existing recordings. A title search by a title-search company is also required. — [Wong, "Errors & Omissions & Rights, Oh My!" IDA, 2012-04-06](https://www.documentary.org/magazine/errors-omissions-rights-oh-my-guide-protecting-your-film)

**What NARA will and will not do (motion pictures)**
- NARA says federal works are treated as public domain, but "not all records in the holdings of the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) are in the public domain." It singles out Special Media holdings. Restrictions can come from copyright, state law, contract, deed restrictions or publicity rights. — [NARA, Motion Pictures: Permissions](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/permissions)
- Where obvious copyright or donor restrictions apply, "written permission from the rights holders may be required" before NARA approves a reproduction. Where status is unknown, NARA releases the item with a warning that "the responsibility for the subsequent use of the materials lies solely with the end user" (36 C.F.R. 1254.62). — [NARA Permissions](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/permissions)
- NARA does not confirm copyright status for any item, does not indemnify users, does not give a definitive public-domain statement, and does not license content or grant usage rights. It can provide accession files, agency catalogs, scripts, production files and deed-of-gift agreements to help with rights research. It also keeps a Source and Permissions Contact List, and it recommends a Copyright Office search or a clearance specialist. — [NARA Permissions](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/permissions)
- NARA's requested credit for audiovisual productions is "Courtesy: National Archives and Records Administration." — [NARA Permissions](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/permissions)
- A restricted example: the Zapruder film at NARA is governed by an agreement that limits copies and requires a copyright notice on all copies. — [JFK ARRB agreement document (via search result)](https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/jfkfiles/NARA-Oct2017/ARRB/S-ADM-G/CATHY/CORRESP/AGREE.WPD.pdf)

**17 U.S.C. §105 and its limits**
- §105(a): "Copyright protection under this title is not available for any work of the United States Government," but the Government may receive and hold copyrights transferred to it. Later subsections (b)–(d) let civilian faculty at specified military and federal academic institutions own copyright in scholarly literary works, subject to a government licence. — [17 U.S.C. §105, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105)
- The House Report says the bar covers works prepared "as part of that person's official duties." An employee who creates a work on their own initiative, outside those duties, can hold copyright even when the subject relates to their government work. — [Cornell LII, §105 notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105)
- For contractor and grantee works, the House Report deliberately avoids a blanket rule. It leaves the question to specific legislation, agency regulations or contract terms, case by case. — [Cornell LII, §105 notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105)
- The House Report also says "work of the United States Government" "does not mean that a work falling within the definition of that term is the property of the U.S. Government." — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105). The US Code site shows Title 17 current through Public Law 119-121, dated October 9, 2026 (from a search-result summary). — [uscode.house.gov §105](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-121/t17/s105/)

**Donated commercial collections inside NARA (Universal Newsreel example)**
- Universal gave its edited newsreels and outtakes to the US through NARA in 1974 "without copyright restrictions." NARA cautions that some stories "may contain other underlying intellectual property or proprietary use rights." The edited newsreels are under description ID 100520 and the outtakes under 559522. Textual "dope sheets" (narration scripts, release sheets, shot lists) fill gaps. — [NARA Unwritten Record, 2013-12-30](https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/2013/12/30/universal-newsreels-national-archives/)
- One secondary source gives 1976 instead of 1974 for the transfer, so dates differ between sources. — [Wikipedia, Universal Newsreel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Newsreel) (search-result summary)

**Archival Producers Alliance (APA)**
- The APA was founded in 2023 and says it represents over 300 archival producers. Its first initiative addressed generative AI in documentary and archival work. — [IDA fiscal-sponsorship page for APA](https://documentary.org/node/40151)
- The APA says it "published the first industry guidelines for filmmakers in 2024" and followed them with a Tool Kit and Case Studies in 2025. Its stated mission is to "ensure that documentary and archival media remain credible, verifiable, and accessible." — [APA GenAI Initiative](https://www.archivalproducersalliance.com/apa-genai-initiative)
- Per search-result summaries, a 2025 APA handbook, "Working with Archival Producers," describes the archival producer's responsibilities across production phases (I did not read the text). — [Doc Voices on the APA](https://purenonfiction.substack.com/p/spotlight-on-archival-producers-alliance); [documentary.org/node/40666](https://documentary.org/node/40666)

### Inferences
- **Status claims cannot be self-certifying.** NARA explicitly refuses to certify status, and the Internet Archive does too (see Q5). An automated system should therefore store a determination together with the evidence behind it, never a bare "PD" flag. The determination should record who made it, when, on what basis, and from which captured records. This fits the existing ledger's rule (<repository>/editvideo/ledger.py) that hedged wording such as "likely public domain" counts as unknown.
- **The §105 basis should be a typed field** with these values: `federal_employee_official_duty`, `contractor_or_grantee` (requires the contract or agency rule as evidence, otherwise unknown), `donated_without_copyright_restrictions` (deed evidence, e.g. Universal 1974), `transferred_to_government` (§105(a) lets the government hold transferred copyrights, so it may not be PD at all), `employee_outside_duties` (not PD), and `unknown`.
- **Proposed clearance-log columns.** I found no authoritative template; this list combines the sourced requirements above with common practice: log ID; programme TC in/out; duration; description; source repository; source IDs (NAID, local identifier, reel, IA identifier); source TC in/out within the source file; element used (picture / sound / both); rights holder; rights basis; licence or agreement reference; fee, term, territory and media; credit line; persons depicted and release status; embedded third-party items; music cue reference; fair-use rationale and opinion-letter reference; status (cleared / pending / replace); reviewer and date.
- Each log row should point to stored evidence files rather than free text. Evidence files include licence PDFs, invoices, deed-of-gift copies, catalog snapshots and Copyright Office search results.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the APA's 2025 "Working with Archival Producers" handbook or any APA archival-practice text with log formats. No authoritative published column standard for an "archival producer's log" was found; the column list above is inference.
- Occidental's sample log is linked from its page, but I did not open it.
- No source specific to motion pictures was found on contractor-produced government films (for example, studio-made WWII training or information films, or agency contractor films). Whether a given film qualifies under §105 depends on contract evidence that I could not survey.

## 2. Errors and Omissions (E&O) insurance: what insurers ask about archival footage, and how fair-use claims are documented

### Takeaway
E&O applications ask whether licences and clearances for film footage, TV clips and photographs, and for persons shown in footage, have been or will be obtained before first release. They also bind the applicant to the insurer's clearance procedures. Insurers accept fair use only with a written fair-use opinion letter from an experienced clearance attorney, and historically only some carriers do so, case by case.

### Cited Findings
- Beazley's producers' E&O application asks whether licences and clearances have been or will be obtained before first release from owners of film footage, TV clips and photographs. It also asks about persons featured in film footage and requires the applicant to follow the clearance procedures attached to the policy (per search-result summary of the PDF). — [Beazley TV/Film E&O application](https://beazley.com/globalassets/product-documents/application/beazley-tv-film-webisodes-theatre-producers-eando-individual-app-ca.pdf)
- A CLE-hosted E&O application asks for a copy of the fair-use opinion letter covering the final cut and the clips used, and whether all licences will be in place before first airing or distribution (per search-result summary). — [UTCLE E&O application](https://utcle.org/ecourses/get-asset-file/asset_id/58080/code/OC9767/)
- "A filmmaker must obtain a fair use opinion letter from an experienced clearance attorney" to rely on fair use. — [Wong, IDA, 2012](https://www.documentary.org/magazine/errors-omissions-rights-oh-my-guide-protecting-your-film)
- Only "a few insurance providers" covered fair use as of 2009. Insurers may want a fuller explanation of the fair-use position and "may even ask you to alter your edit before they will agree to insure your film." — [Rawady, IDA, 2009](https://documentary.org/node/34395)
- Between January and May 2007, four major documentary E&O insurers (AIG, MediaPro, ChubbPro, OneBeacon) announced programmes covering fair-use claims. All required a legal opinion letter, and the Documentary Filmmakers' Statement of Best Practices in Fair Use made those letters easier to write. — [EFF Deeplinks, 2007](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2007/02/film-insurers-recognize-fair-use); [IDA, "Fair Use Put to Good Use"](https://documentary.org/node/34824); [Berkman Klein, "Insurers Start to Recognize Fair Use"](https://cyber.harvard.edu/node/93519)
- Getting E&O coverage can take at least 3–5 days, and longer if rights, releases, title report or music clearances are missing. — [IDA, "Quick, I just got a distribution deal…"](https://documentary.org/node/34950)
- Festivals, broadcasters and streamers require clearance warranties. A missing clearance can block festival entry, void distribution contracts or void E&O coverage (EU/Italy-focused guide). — [D&I Media, "Clearing Copyrighted Material"](https://dandi.media/en/clearing-copyrighted-material/)

### Inferences
- **Two evidence packages.** The system should export (a) a "clearance package" per cut: the clearance log, licences and invoices, catalog captures, determination memos, the music cue sheet, releases and the credit list, and (b) a separate "fair-use dossier" for any clip whose basis is fair use. The dossier would hold the clip, its duration, its purpose in the film (what the commentary or critique is about), why that amount was needed, the alternatives considered, and the attorney opinion-letter reference. Fair-use clips should be marked `insurer_dependent` and should block delivery to broadcast and streaming until an opinion letter is attached.
- **E&O questions as gates.** For example: "every footage row has a rights basis with evidence", "every recognizable living person in footage is either released or flagged for attorney review", "every music occurrence is cleared or removed."
- Because insurers may require edit changes, fair-use clips need non-destructive replacement slots in the timeline, so a reviewer can swap a clip without re-cutting the sequence.

### Gaps
- I found no current (2025–2026) information on which carriers accept fair-use opinion letters, or on current premiums and terms. The coverage I found is from 2007–2012.
- I could not read any insurer's actual "clearance procedures" attachment, which is the binding document. The requirements here come from secondary IDA articles and application-form summaries.
- The Center for Media & Social Impact's current fair-use code (and any revision after 2005) was not retrieved in this session.

## 3. Detecting third-party content inside public-domain films (music tracks, logos, embedded clips; audio rights vs picture rights)

### Takeaway
Public-domain picture does not make the soundtrack public domain, and a reel that is "free of copyright restrictions" can still contain embedded third-party material. NARA says this explicitly for Universal Newsreel. The audio, especially music, is the main risk and has to be cleared separately from the picture.

### Cited Findings
- Universal Newsreel's 1974 donation was "without copyright restrictions," but stories "may contain other underlying intellectual property or proprietary use rights." Universal discarded many separate music and narration soundtracks from 1929–1956 before the donation. A 1978 nitrate fire destroyed most of volumes 14–17 (1941–45). — [NARA Unwritten Record](https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/2013/12/30/universal-newsreels-national-archives/)
- Per search-result summaries of NARA pages, the catalog shows no access restrictions for Universal Newsreel records, but the description warns that "some or all of these materials may be restricted by copyright or other intellectual property right restrictions." Individual reels may include third-party copyrighted content. — [NARA, Newsreels at College Park](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/newsreels); [Unwritten Record, Universal Newsreel descriptions](https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/?p=29079)
- NARA tells users to determine whether all or part of a work is copyrighted, whether protection has lapsed, and "whether performance-based or other rights are claimed." — [NARA Permissions](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/permissions)
- Insurer-style procedures treat music separately: rights for commissioned music and for pre-existing music or recordings. — [Wong, IDA 2012](https://www.documentary.org/magazine/errors-omissions-rights-oh-my-guide-protecting-your-film)
- Incidental background music must be logged. — [Occidental College](https://www.oxy.edu/media-arts-culture/student-resources/production-handbook/shooting-your-project/producing-rights)

### Inferences
- **Separate rights fields per stem.** Each clip should carry `picture_rights`, `music_composition_rights`, `sound_recording_rights`, `narration_rights` and `embedded_items[]`, each with its own basis and evidence. The default for silent-picture use of a PD reel is picture = PD and audio = "not used". The edit should record that the original audio was muted or dropped, so the music question never comes up.
- **Detection pipeline:**
  - (a) Audio fingerprinting of the source audio against a music recognition service. Treat any match as an embedded item requiring clearance.
  - (b) Music/speech classification to flag any music bed even when no match is found.
  - (c) Logo and bug detection, plus OCR on frames, for network bugs, studio cards, "© year" title cards and newsreel company title cards. A newsreel card from a commercial company inside a government compilation is a red flag.
  - (d) Shot-boundary and texture/grain discontinuity checks to find inserted footage of a different generation or gauge inside a reel. This often marks a borrowed clip.
  - (e) Face detection, flagging recognizable people for publicity review.
  - Every hit becomes an `embedded_items[]` entry with source-file frame ranges.
- Background knowledge, not verified this session (an attorney should confirm):
  - Pre-1972 US sound recordings received federal protection under the 2018 Music Modernization Act (CLASSICS Act), with staggered expiry. Compositions and recordings therefore have separate terms.
  - Under the 95-year rule, US works published in 1930 or earlier are in the US public domain as of January 1, 2026.
  - Both points matter for newsreel music, but the music must still be identified first.

### Gaps
- I found no published industry procedure, from APA, IDA or an insurer, that specifies automated detection methods for embedded third-party content. The pipeline above is inference.
- I did not retrieve a source describing which agency-produced films (for example WWII "Why We Fight" or NASA films) are known to contain licensed commercial footage or music.

## 4. Content ID and YouTube: public-domain footage claimed by others; dispute process and evidence

### Takeaway
YouTube's dispute form lists three valid grounds: you have all necessary rights, the use is a copyright exception such as fair use, or the content was misidentified. "Public domain" is not a listed ground, and the help page asks for no evidence. Claimants have 30 days to respond to a dispute and 7 days for an appeal. Public-domain government footage has been claimed through Content ID by media companies, so a channel needs its evidence ready before it publishes.

### Cited Findings
- Valid dispute reasons: you have all the necessary rights; the use qualifies as a copyright exception such as fair use; "Believing your video was misidentified or an error was made." Invalid reasons include giving credit, owning a copy, or not monetizing. The page only says you "may want to learn more about public domain" and does not list it as a reason. — [YouTube Help, Dispute a Content ID claim](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797454?hl=en)
- Timelines: the claimant has 30 days to respond, or the claim "will expire and be released." A rejected dispute may be appealed, with 7 days for the claimant. "Escalate to Appeal" applies only to claims that block a video, also 7 days. A dispute cannot be cancelled. The claimant may file a takedown at any time, and a valid takedown brings a copyright strike. "Repeated or malicious abuse of the dispute process can result in penalties." Dispute only "if you're confident that you have all the necessary rights." — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797454?hl=en)
- In 2011, reporting reposted on a mailing list said FedFlix, a distributor of US government films, had hundreds of public-domain videos claimed through Content ID by companies including NBC Universal, Al Jazeera and Discovery. — [NCUC mailing list repost, Dec 2011](https://lists.ncuc.org/pipermail/ncuc-discuss/2011-December/013833.html) (secondary)
- Digital Trends reported Content ID claims on NASA footage, which is public domain, by claimants who did not own it (per search-result summary). — [Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/?p=368347)

### Inferences
- **Which ground to choose.** For PD footage the realistic grounds are "I have all the necessary rights" or "misidentified / error." This is my inference, since YouTube does not map PD to a ground. The free-text explanation should cite the specific source item and its basis, for example: "NARA Local Identifier 111-LC-xxxxx, NAID nnnn; work of the US Government, 17 U.S.C. §105; catalog page captured 2026-10-27 shows no use restrictions."
- **Evidence pack per published video,** generated from the ledger before upload:
  - for each claimable segment: video TC range → source item → catalog URL and identifiers → captured catalog snapshot (with hash and capture time) → §105 or deed basis → retrieval record (URL, date, file hash) → whether audio was used;
  - plus credits as shown in the video.
- **Remastered copies.** A claimant may hold a restored or remastered copy of the same PD material. The system should record which copy we used (repository file hash). That way the dispute can say our footage comes from the repository's own transfer, not the claimant's version.
- **Penalty risk.** Because the dispute process carries penalties, the system should not dispute automatically. It should prepare the text and evidence and leave the submission to a human.
- **Pre-flight check.** Unlisted uploads give an early read on claims before public release (common creator practice, not verified as YouTube guidance this session).

### Gaps
- I did not verify YouTube's current Content ID eligibility rules on whether rightsholders may reference public-domain material. That page was not retrieved.
- I found no 2024–2026 data on how often PD claims are released after dispute. The cited cases are from about 2011.

## 5. Technical provenance: original files, hashes, catalog identifiers and URLs, capture dates, citing NARA and Internet Archive items, frame-accurate source timecodes in an EDL

### Takeaway
The repositories identify items by catalog numbers: NARA uses record group, local identifier and reel, and NARA's catalog also has numeric description IDs; the Internet Archive uses item identifiers. Neither repository certifies rights. The system therefore has to preserve the original file, a cryptographic hash, the catalog record as it looked when captured, and frame-accurate mapping from each edit event back to the original file's frames.

### Cited Findings
- NARA reproduction orders require item-level citations: record group number, Local Identifier (item control number), title or subject, and reel number for multi-reel items. Examples include "111-LC-44332" and "H-HF-112X." — [NARA, Order copies of moving image and sound holdings](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/order-copies-of-motion-pictures-sound-recordings-and-video)
- NARA transfers whole reels ("an entire reel or item must be transferred, not individual clips or scenes") through approved vendors (Colorlab, Henninger Media Services, Video & Film Solutions). Copies come from Intermediate (I) or Preservation (P) elements, or an Electronic Intermediate (EI) at the requester's expense. Approvals are valid for 6 months. — [NARA Order copies](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/order-copies-of-motion-pictures-sound-recordings-and-video)
- NARA's catalog uses numeric description IDs; for example, Universal Newsreel edited newsreels are ID 100520 and outtakes 559522. — [NARA Unwritten Record](https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/2013/12/30/universal-newsreels-national-archives/)
- The Internet Archive "does not make guarantees as to the copyright status of items on archive.org" and "cannot guarantee information posted on item details or collection pages." Rights information comes from uploaders, who often supply it in the description or through a Creative Commons licence choice. Users use the collections "at their own risk." — [Internet Archive Help, Rights](https://help.archive.org/help/rights/)
- Prelinger Archives on the Internet Archive: Wikipedia says over 8,500 public-domain films are available for download and unrestricted reuse, and that the films can also be licensed through Getty Images (search-result summary). — [Wikipedia, Prelinger Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prelinger_Archives). In 2001, Rick Prelinger wrote that he donated online distribution rights for the films. — [nettime, 2001-04-22](https://www.nettime.org/Lists-Archives/nettime-l-0104/msg00106.html). In a 2002 declaration he said reuse of the digitized films was left unrestricted. — [Prelinger declaration, EFF](https://w2.eff.org/IP/P2P/MGM_v_Grokster/20020122_prelinger_decl.html). One 2005 forum post said the Archive's terms page at the time limited use to scholarship and research. Terms may therefore have differed between item labels and site terms. — [Vegas forum post (secondary)](https://www.vegascreativesoftware.info/us/forum/wot-the-forumator--38519)

### Inferences
- **Proposed data model for rights evidence** (my synthesis; field names are suggestions, extending <repository>/editvideo/ledger.py):
  - **SourceItem** (one per catalog item or reel)
    - `repository` (NARA | IA | LoC | other)
    - `ids`: {`naid`, `local_identifier`, `record_group`, `series_naid`, `reel`, `ia_identifier`, `ia_collection`}
    - `catalog_url`
    - `catalog_snapshot`: {`path` to saved HTML or PDF plus a screenshot, `captured_utc`, `sha256`, `fields_verbatim`: {`use_restrictions`, `access_restrictions`, `rights`/`licenseurl`, `creator`, `producer`, `date`, `credits`}}
    - `creator_type`: federal_official_duty | contractor_grantee | donated_commercial | transferred_to_gov | private | foreign | unknown
    - `rights_basis_picture`: {type (§105, deed_no_restriction, term_expired, CC0, CC-BY, licensed, fair_use, unknown), evidence[], determined_by, determined_on, rationale}
    - `rights_basis_audio`: same shape, kept separate
    - `embedded_items[]`
    - `persons_depicted[]`
    - `credit_line` (for example NARA's "Courtesy: National Archives and Records Administration")
  - **SourceFile** (one per downloaded or delivered file)
    - `source_item`
    - `retrieval`: {`url`, `retrieved_utc`, HTTP `etag` / `last_modified` / `content_length`, `tool`}
    - `sha256`, `bytes`
    - `probe`: {container, codec, width × height, fps, field_order, timecode track}
    - `generation`: for example "IA derivative h.264 of uploader MPEG-2" or "NARA EI ProRes 422 HQ 2K from Intermediate"
    - `vendor`, `original_preserved_path` (read-only)
  - **Derivative** (each processing step)
    - `parent_sha256`, `child_sha256`
    - `operations[]`: {op, params, tool + version, model + version for ML}
    - `alters_content`: bool (true for upscaling, colorization, frame interpolation, inpainting)
  - **EditEvent** (EDL row)
    - `event`, `record_in` / `record_out` (programme TC)
    - `source_file_sha256`, `source_in` / `source_out` as frame numbers in the original file plus the source timecode or source fps
    - `uses_picture`, `uses_audio`
    - `derivative_chain`, `onscreen_label`
    - `rights_status_at_lock`, `cleared_for`: internal | online | broadcast
- **Frame-accurate source timecodes.** Store the source in/out as integer frame indices of the original preserved file, not only as seconds in a re-encoded proxy. Store the source's native fps alongside them. A conform or a dispute can then point to exact frames even after cadence removal changes the frame count; keep a map from original to decimated frames.
- **Original file and catalog snapshot.** Keep the original, hashed, untouched. Re-capture the catalog record at download time and again at picture lock, and alert if the use-restriction fields changed in between.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve NARA's official citation guidance for motion pictures. The order page uses Local Identifiers and does not mention the term "NAID," so the exact NARA-preferred citation format for productions is not confirmed here.
- I did not retrieve Internet Archive metadata API documentation (item identifier and `licenseurl` field). Treating the archive.org/details/<identifier> path as the citable ID is my inference.
- I found no industry source prescribing hashing or snapshot practice for documentaries. That recommendation draws on general digital-preservation practice and is not sourced here.

## 6. Quality practices for public-repository footage (generation loss, best available scan, deinterlacing and cadence removal, labelling restored or upscaled footage)

### Takeaway
Repository downloads are often access derivatives several generations from the film element. NARA's paid route delivers whole-reel transfers up to 4K DPX/ProRes, but quality is "limited by the quality of the original film." For cadence, footage shot at 16–18 fps and transferred at 24 or 30 fps carries repeated frames, which can be detected and removed (for example 3 unique frames in every 4 at 24 fps for 18 fps material) before the real speed is set. For labelling, the APA's 2024 guidance calls for transparency and audience disclosure of generated or altered material, but I could not read the full text.

### Cited Findings
- NARA reproduction formats include "DPX, ProRes422HQ, ProRes4444; in 4k, 2k," 16 mm and 35 mm film, and SD/HD video. "The resultant copy will reflect the variations in quality that are in the originals." Commercial quality "meets F.C.C. standards (limited by the quality of the original film)." Exhibition and home copies are "fair to good" and videotapes "may contain a visible flicker." Defects inherent in the original are not grounds for return. NARA no longer has a vendor for broadcast-quality sound reproductions. — [NARA Order copies](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/order-copies-of-motion-pictures-sound-recordings-and-video)
- A Pending code of "P" means no intermediate copy exists, and NARA may require an Electronic Intermediate at the requester's expense; "I" means an intermediate exists and is sent to the vendor. - Cadence: practitioners note that a 1-in-4 repeat pattern at 24 fps means 18 fps source (0.75 × 24 = 18). AviSynth's TDecimate (Cycle=4, CycleR=1) or FDecimate removes the repeats, and playback speed is then set separately, for example with AssumeFPS. FDecimate "preferentially removes duplicate frames" and allows output rates that are not 1-in-N. If telecine also added duplicates, IVTC may drop the wrong frames, so check where the duplicates fall first. — [Doom9 forum thread](https://forum.doom9.org/showthread.php?p=1644738); [Doom9 "Decimating while removing duplicates"](https://forum.doom9.org/archive/index.php/t-184857.html); [AviSynth FDecimate docs](https://avisynth.org.ru/docs/english/externalfilters/fdecimate.htm); [VideoHelp forum](https://forum.videohelp.com/showthread.php?p=2765364) (forum sources; summarized from search results)

### Inferences
- **Best-available-source selection rule:**
  - Prefer the repository's highest-generation file: NARA catalog master or ordered EI/ProRes, ahead of IA original upload, ahead of IA derivative MP4, ahead of third-party YouTube re-uploads. Never use YouTube re-uploads as a source, because they break the chain of custody and may themselves be claimed.
  - Record `generation` and the source element (I or P copy) on the SourceFile.
  - When several copies exist, score them on resolution, compression (bits per pixel), presence of burned-in timecode or watermarks, and field structure, and keep the losers' hashes as "considered."
- **Cadence QC steps** (inference; filter behaviour per ffmpeg documentation, which I could not re-verify this session because the fetch failed):
  - (1) Run interlace detection (ffmpeg `idet`) to separate truly interlaced material, which needs bob or weave deinterlacing (`bwdif`/`yadif`), from telecined progressive film, which needs field matching (`fieldmatch`) followed by decimation (`decimate`) or `pullup`.
  - (2) After that, detect repeated whole frames with a frame-difference trace (for example `mpdecimate`-style thresholds or per-frame SSIM to the previous frame) and estimate the repeat pattern. 1 repeat in 4 at 24 fps suggests 18 fps; 1 in 3 suggests 16 fps.
  - (3) Remove the repeats and re-time to the true rate, then conform to the delivery rate with a cadence that hides the conversion, rather than leaving uneven judder.
  - (4) Store the pattern found, the frames dropped and the original-to-new frame map in the Derivative record, so EDL source frames stay traceable.
- **Labelling restored or upscaled footage.** Separate operations that only recover what is in the frame from those that synthesize content:
  - Recover only: transfer, stabilization, dust-busting, gamma and levels, cadence removal. These need a `restored` note in the log only.
  - Synthesize: ML upscaling, frame interpolation, colorization, inpainting, face restoration. Set `alters_content = true`, record model and version, add an on-screen or credits disclosure ("Upscaled/colorized; not original"), and keep the unaltered version available for reviewers.
  - This follows the APA's transparency direction as summarized, but the exact APA wording on restoration was not verified.
- The existing ledger already carries a "generated → label as illustration (AI or 3D)" duty. A parallel duty for "altered archival" would cover upscaled or colorized real footage, which is neither generated nor untouched.
